About

I am a Max Weber Fellow at the School of Transnational Governance, European University Institute, Florence. Trained as an interdisciplinary social scientist with a background in law and governance, my doctoral research at the Centre for the Study of Law and Governance, Jawaharlal Nehru University, New Delhi, examined environmental cooperative behaviour as a social norm in India, taking household plastics recycling in urban India as a case for asking whether, and under what conditions, norm-based regulation can govern cleaning behaviour where formal enforcement is thin.

That work sits within a broader interest in behavioural public policy and the regulation of behaviour. I am now extending an earlier MPhil engagement with law and technology into questions of repair, the internal-combustion-to-electric-vehicle transition, automation, and blue-collar work, with particular attention to China and its wider consequences.

Across these cases the analytical commitments are consistent. Explanations are paired rather than monocausal; outcomes are read as structural outputs of an institutional configuration rather than as the realised intentions of its designers; and the distributional question, who gains capability and who is left holding a skill that no longer clears, is never deferred to a later section. The empirical anchor is India and, more broadly, the Global South.

Research

Norm management authority, waste, and caste

Source-segregation compliance in Indian cities is not primarily a behavioural problem solvable by nudges. It is a question of who holds the authority to manage a norm, and of the social, specifically caste, conditions under which that authority is granted or denied. Fieldwork in Aurangabad (Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar) sits alongside a five-city comparison of segregation performance against municipal solid-waste financing.

Repair, tacit skill, and the ICE → EV transition

The move from the internal-combustion engine to the electric vehicle is not a change of propulsion but a change in the human capital beneath the product. The mechanic, the corner parts shop, the second-hand component market and the informal apprenticeship pipeline were not services attached to the vehicle; they were the institution. A Delhi repair cluster, Nehru Place, is the site where that dissolution can already be read, and generative AI is run in parallel as the cognitive case of the same mechanism: the machine absorbs the codifiable layer, and value concentrates in the non-codifiable residue.

Institutional sources of industrial capability

China's paradigm succession from mechanical to electronics engineering, visible in the new energy vehicle industry, is treated as the structural output of a specific configuration: dense agglomeration paired with decentralised competitive market institutions. The electronics reservoir underwriting NEVs was built through smartphones and consumer durables, which makes the long hybrid governance form between lead firms and Chinese suppliers, neither arm's-length contracting nor vertical integration, the object worth explaining.

Supply-chain governance and the assembly tier

Apple's shift of iPhone production to India is easy to misread as a cheap-labour story. Two paired mechanisms do the work instead: tariff exposure makes the location of final assembly a matter of geopolitical arbitrage, and automation has emptied that tier of the tacit capacity that once made offshoring risky. Relocation registers as upgrading for the receiving economy while the capability that compounds stays where it was built.

Urban commons and the care of animals

Urbanisation has severed a long-standing relation between households and the animals around them. Restoring it is a matter of civic re-moralisation and the decentralisation of care rather than of further state regulation, which reframes the Indian stray-dog question as a commons problem rather than a nuisance-control one, with the municipal cost structure of sterilisation against shelters as the distributive test.
